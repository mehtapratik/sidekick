Most productivity software is built for a team. It assumes a manager who wants visibility, a workflow that has to fit twenty people, and fields that exist because a report needs them. A person using it alone inherits all of that weight and none of the reasons for it.

Sidekick is built for one person. Every module adapts to how that individual thinks and works, not to how a team should coordinate. The user sits in the decision maker's seat: they choose which fields an entry carries, which links matter, what counts as a change and when to act on one. The system can suggest. The user decides.

In practice, nothing the user fills in is mandatory. A plan, a chat session or a one-sentence datum carries only the metadata its owner, or the bot that produced it, finds useful. Sidekick keeps its own bookkeeping (ids, counters, timestamps) out of the user's way, so the structure never costs them effort.

This principle has a limit. Sharing, a public profile and published posts may come, but they serve the individual and take second place to the personal workflow. Teamwork and collaboration are out of scope for the next four to five years. When a feature would trade one person's control for a group's convenience, the person wins.
